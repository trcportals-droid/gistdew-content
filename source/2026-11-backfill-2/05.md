**Quick gist:** The UK allows dual citizenship, so it won't ask you to give up your Nigerian nationality when you naturalise. Under Nigeria's constitution, a person who is a Nigerian citizen by birth generally keeps that citizenship when they acquire another one. So most Nigerians born in Nigeria can hold both passports. People who became Nigerian by registration or naturalisation are in a different position, so check your own case.

## What the UK says

The UK has no rule against holding more than one nationality. When you naturalise as a British citizen, you don't have to renounce your Nigerian citizenship. GOV.UK confirms this, though it also notes that some other countries do not allow dual nationality, so you should check the rules of your other country.

## What Nigeria says

Nigeria's 1999 Constitution deals with this in its citizenship chapter. In short:

- **Nigerians by birth** can hold another citizenship without losing their Nigerian citizenship.
- **Nigerians by registration or naturalisation** can lose Nigerian citizenship if they acquire another nationality, unless it was acquired by birth.

Most Nigerians in the UK were born Nigerian, so they fall into the first group. If you're unsure which applies to you, speak to a Nigerian lawyer.

## Your passports in practice

Once you're a dual citizen:

- **Leaving and entering the UK:** use your British passport.
- **Entering and leaving Nigeria:** many dual nationals use their Nigerian passport, which avoids the need for a Nigerian visa. If you travel to Nigeria on your British passport, you'll need a visa or another valid entry document.
- **Keep both valid.** Renewing your Nigerian passport from the UK takes time, so don't let it lapse if you plan to visit home.

## What about your children?

Children born in the UK to a Nigerian parent are usually Nigerian by birth too, as Nigerian citizenship passes through a parent. They may also be British, depending on the parents' status at the time of birth. Many families get both passports for their children, which makes visits home easier.

## Before you naturalise

Becoming British is a big step. Check that you meet the requirements, including the residence period, good character rules and the Life in the UK test. Applying costs a significant fee, so make sure you qualify first.

## Common myths

- **"You'll lose your Nigerian citizenship automatically."** Not if you're Nigerian by birth.
- **"You must declare your British passport at Lagos airport."** Use one passport consistently for each country, and carry both when travelling.
- **"You can't own land in Nigeria once you're British."** As a Nigerian citizen, you keep your rights. Foreign nationals face more restrictions.

## The bottom line

For most Nigerians by birth, becoming British doesn't mean giving up being Nigerian. Keep both passports valid and use the right one at each border.

## Five titles, five generations

**1985 (China):** Nigeria won the first-ever edition of the tournament, then called the U-16 World Championship. It announced Nigeria as a youth football powerhouse.

**1993 (Japan):** A talented squad including a young Nwankwo Kanu won the title. Kanu would go on to win the Champions League and Olympic gold.

**2007 (South Korea):** Nigeria beat Spain on penalties in the final, with a squad that included several future professionals.

**2013 (UAE):** One of the most exciting Eaglets teams ever. They scored freely, and striker Kelechi Iheanacho won the Golden Ball as the tournament's best player.

**2015 (Chile):** Nigeria defended its title, with Victor Osimhen finishing as top scorer. He later became one of Africa's most famous strikers.

## Why Nigeria does so well at this level

- **Huge talent pool:** football is everywhere, from street games to academies.
- **Early physical development:** many observers point out that Nigerian teenagers often play with strength and pace that stand out at youth level.
- **Hunger:** for many young players, the tournament is a direct route to scouts from top European clubs.

## The age question

Nigeria's youth success has sometimes been shadowed by concerns about age falsification, a problem in youth football in many countries. FIFA introduced MRI wrist scans to help check players' ages at U-17 level. Honest age-grade football protects young players and keeps competitions fair.

## From Eaglets to Super Eagles

Not every U-17 star makes it as a senior player. The jump is huge, and injuries, poor advice and bad transfers derail many careers. But the stars who do make it, like Kanu, Iheanacho and Osimhen, show what's possible.

## How diaspora fans can follow

Youth tournaments are usually streamed online through FIFA's platforms or partner broadcasters. They're a great way to spot future stars before everyone else does.

## The bottom line

Five world titles at U-17 level is an extraordinary record. The challenge for Nigerian football is turning that youth success into senior trophies.

**Quick gist:** UK ENIC is the UK's national agency for recognising international qualifications. For a fee, it issues a Statement of Comparability showing how your Nigerian degree, diploma or certificate compares to UK levels. It isn't always required, but it can help with jobs, further study and some visa routes.

## What UK ENIC does

UK ENIC (formerly UK NARIC) compares qualifications from other countries with the UK system. A Statement of Comparability might say, for example, that your Nigerian bachelor's degree is comparable to a UK bachelor's degree. Employers, universities and professional bodies sometimes ask for this.

## When you might need it

- **Job applications:** some employers, especially in the public sector, ask for proof that your qualifications are at a certain level.
- **Further study:** UK universities usually assess international qualifications themselves, but a statement can help.
- **Visa routes:** some immigration routes accept qualifications as evidence of English language ability or skill level, and UK ENIC confirmation may be needed. Check the guidance for your route.
- **Teaching and other professions:** some professional routes require an ENIC statement as part of the process.

## When you probably don't need it

Many private-sector employers are happy to look at your CV and qualifications directly. Regulated professions like nursing, medicine and law have their own processes through bodies like the NMC, GMC and SRA, so check what they require before paying for an ENIC statement.

## How to apply

1. Create an account on the UK ENIC website.
2. Choose the service you need, usually the Statement of Comparability.
3. Upload your certificates and transcripts. You'll usually need clear scans of originals, plus certified translations if they're not in English.
4. Pay the fee.
5. Wait for the assessment. Fast-track options cost more.

## Tips to avoid delays

- **Use your full name consistently** across your documents, or explain any differences.
- **Include transcripts.** They help the assessors understand your course.
- **Order documents early.** Getting transcripts from some Nigerian universities can take time, so start before you need the statement.

## Make it work on your CV

Once you have the statement, add a short line to your CV such as: "BSc Accounting, University of Lagos (UK ENIC: comparable to UK bachelor's degree)." It helps recruiters who don't know Nigerian institutions.

## The bottom line

A UK ENIC statement isn't always necessary, but it can remove doubt for employers and institutions. Check whether your target job or route asks for one before paying.

**Quick gist:** Start your budget from your take-home pay, not your salary. Cover fixed costs first (rent, council tax, bills, transport), then set aside savings and family support as fixed amounts, and spend what's left. A simple split, reviewed monthly, works better than a perfect spreadsheet you never open.

## Step 1: know your real income

Your payslip shows your gross pay, but you live on your net pay after tax, National Insurance and pension. Use your net monthly pay as your starting number. If your hours vary, use a cautious average.

## Step 2: list your fixed costs

These are the bills you can't avoid each month:

- Rent
- Council tax
- Energy (gas and electricity)
- Water
- Phone and internet
- Transport (bus pass, train season ticket or car costs)
- Insurance
- Debt repayments

Add them up. If they take more than about two-thirds of your take-home pay, things will feel tight, and it's worth looking at cheaper housing or bills.

## Step 3: pay yourself and your family on purpose

Decide two fixed amounts at the start of the month:

- **Savings:** even £50 a month builds a cushion over time. Aim for an emergency fund covering a few months' essential costs.
- **Money home:** choose a realistic monthly amount for family support, rather than reacting to every request.

Set up standing orders on payday so this happens automatically.

## Step 4: spend the rest with a cap

Food, toiletries, clothes and fun come from what's left. Many people find a weekly limit easier to track than a monthly one.

## A simple example

If your take-home pay is £2,000 a month:

- Fixed costs: £1,250
- Savings: £150
- Money home: £200
- Day-to-day spending: £400 (about £90 a week)

Your numbers will differ, but the structure stays the same.

## Easy ways to save

- **Shop at discount supermarkets** and use loyalty schemes.
- **Batch cook** Nigerian stews and soups and freeze portions.
- **Switch energy and phone deals** when contracts end.
- **Use a railcard** if you travel by train often.
- **Avoid buy-now-pay-later** for everyday items; small payments add up.

## Review every month

Check your bank statement once a month. Look for subscriptions you forgot, bills that went up and spending that crept up. Adjust next month's plan.

## The bottom line

A budget doesn't have to be complicated. Know your take-home pay, cover your fixed costs, pay your savings and family first, and give yourself a weekly spending limit.

**Quick gist:** Fuji is a Yoruba popular music style built on talking drums, percussion and powerful vocals. It grew out of were music, the songs sung to wake Muslims for the pre-dawn meal during Ramadan, and was popularised from the 1960s and 1970s by Sikiru Ayinde Barrister. Today it's a staple of owambes in Nigeria and across the diaspora.

## Where fuji came from

During Ramadan, groups of young singers would go round neighbourhoods before dawn singing were (also spelled ajisari) songs to wake people for sahur, the meal before the fast. Over time, singers developed these songs into a full performance style with drums and call-and-response vocals.

Sikiru Ayinde Barrister is widely credited with shaping and naming fuji. He said he took the name from Mount Fuji in Japan, which he'd seen on a poster and liked the sound of.

## What it sounds like

Fuji is dominated by percussion: talking drums (dundun), sakara drums, shekere and other instruments. The lead singer delivers long, improvised vocals, praising guests, telling stories and teasing rivals, while backing singers answer. Songs can run for a long time as the band follows the energy of the crowd.

## The big names

Beyond Barrister, fuji has produced many stars, including Ayinde Kollington, Wasiu Ayinde Marshal (K1 De Ultimate), Saheed Osupa and Pasuma. Rivalries between musicians and their fans have long been part of fuji culture.

## Fuji and the diaspora

At Yoruba weddings, birthdays and funerals in London, Houston or Toronto, a fuji musician or a DJ playing fuji is often a must. Big names tour abroad regularly, especially in summer and around December. For many people, fuji is the sound of home.

## Fuji meets Afrobeats

Many Afrobeats stars grew up on fuji, and you can hear its rhythms, vocal style and Yoruba wordplay in modern songs. Some artists have collaborated with fuji legends, introducing the sound to younger fans.

## How to get into fuji

- Search for fuji classics and live recordings on streaming platforms.
- Ask your parents or uncles for their favourite album; they'll have opinions.
- Watch live performances on YouTube to understand the energy.

## The bottom line

Fuji grew from Ramadan wake-up songs into one of Nigeria's most loved party sounds. If you've been to a Yoruba owambe, you've almost certainly danced to it.

## Who can apply

You can usually apply as a partner if your partner is:

- A British or Irish citizen
- Settled in the UK (for example, has Indefinite Leave to Remain or settled status)

- The UK partner's employment income
- Self-employment income

## Why it feels so hard

Back home, your friendships were built over years: school, church, family and neighbourhood. Abroad, you start from zero, often while working long hours. It's normal to feel isolated for the first months. It doesn't mean you made the wrong decision.

## Be in the same place regularly

Research and experience both suggest friendships grow from repeated, casual contact. So rather than one big event, choose two or three things you'll do every week.

## Where to meet people

- **Faith communities:** African churches and mosques are often the strongest support networks for new arrivals. Many have welcome teams, youth groups and fellowships.

**Quick gist:** Many Nigerians abroad send goods home using cargo companies that consolidate shipments by sea (cheaper, slower) or air (faster, more expensive). Choose a reputable company, get a written quote including delivery and clearing, pack carefully and check Nigeria's prohibited and restricted items list before you send anything.

## Sea vs air

- **Sea cargo:** cheaper for heavy or bulky items like barrels, furniture and appliances. It can take several weeks, plus time at the port and for customs clearing.
- **Air cargo:** faster, often a week or two, but priced by weight or size. Better for smaller, urgent or valuable items.

## How the process usually works

1. **Get quotes** from a few cargo companies. Ask what's included: collection, shipping, customs clearing, duties and delivery to the final address.
2. **Pack and label** your items. Include a clear packing list.
3. **Drop off or arrange collection.**
4. **Track the shipment** and stay in touch with the company.
5. **Collection or delivery** in Nigeria, often from the company's warehouse in Lagos or another city.

## What to check before choosing a company

- How long they've been operating and their reviews
- A physical address in both countries
- Written terms covering delays, damage and loss
- Whether they offer insurance

Be wary of very cheap quotes and companies that only exist on social media.

## Prohibited and restricted items

Nigeria bans or restricts the import of some goods. Restrictions can change, so check the Nigeria Customs Service website and ask your cargo company. Common problem areas include certain food items, medicines, and some used items. Never send anything illegal or anything you're unsure about.

## Customs duties

Duties depend on what you're sending and its value. Some companies include clearing in their price; others charge separately. Get it in writing to avoid surprise charges.

## Packing tips

- Use strong barrels or boxes and seal them well.
- Pack heavier items at the bottom.
- Wrap electronics and keep receipts.
- Write a list of contents and keep photos.
- Don't overpack; damaged barrels cause problems.

## Plan for December

Before Christmas and Detty December, cargo companies get busy and prices can rise. Ship early if you want goods to arrive in time.

## The bottom line

Shipping goods home is common and can save money, but choose a trusted company, understand what's included and check the rules before you pack.

## Early juju

Juju grew in Lagos in the 1920s and 1930s among small groups playing guitar, banjo and percussion. Tunde King is often credited as an early pioneer. Over time, bands grew and added talking drums and more guitars.

## I.K. Dairo and the modern sound

In the 1950s and 1960s, I.K. Dairo brought accordion, talking drums and polished arrangements to juju, making it hugely popular. His success helped juju become the music of Yoruba social life.

## King Sunny Adé and Ebenezer Obey

From the late 1960s, two bandleaders dominated juju:

- **King Sunny Adé** added pedal steel guitar, more electric guitars and a dreamy, layered sound. His 1980s international releases led to tours abroad and global recognition, including Grammy nominations.
- **Ebenezer Obey** blended juju with Christian themes and storytelling, building a huge following.

Their friendly rivalry gave fans decades of great music.

## What juju sounds like

Expect interlocking guitars, talking drums, shakers and call-and-response vocals. Songs often praise hosts and guests and carry proverbs and moral messages. Like fuji, performances can stretch on as long as the party continues.

## Juju today

Juju still plays at weddings and celebrations, and younger musicians mix its guitar style into Afrobeats and gospel. Many diaspora parties still feature juju bands or classic juju playlists.

## Where to start listening

Search for King Sunny Adé, Ebenezer Obey and I.K. Dairo on streaming services. Ask older relatives for their favourite songs; they'll probably have a story attached.

## The bottom line

Juju is the elegant, guitar-led cousin of fuji and one of the foundations of modern Nigerian music. If you love the guitar lines in today's Afrobeats, you'll hear where they came from.
