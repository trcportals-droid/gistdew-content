**Quick gist:** For decades, the Nigerian government kept petrol prices artificially low by paying a subsidy. President Bola Tinubu announced its removal in his inauguration speech in May 2023. Pump prices rose sharply, transport and food costs followed, and many families leaned more heavily on relatives abroad. Supporters say it freed money for other priorities; critics point to the hardship it caused.

## What the subsidy was

Nigeria is a major oil producer but for years imported most of its refined petrol. The government paid the difference between the market cost and a fixed, lower pump price. Over time, the cost grew enormously, and critics said it was wasteful and open to fraud.

## What changed in 2023

When the subsidy ended, petrol prices rose quickly. Because transport costs affect almost everything, prices of food, goods and services also went up. Around the same time, changes to the foreign exchange system led to a sharp fall in the naira, which pushed import costs higher too.

## The arguments for removal

- The subsidy cost the government huge sums each year.
- Much of the benefit went to wealthier people and smugglers.
- Removing it could free money for health, education and infrastructure.
- Many economists and international bodies had long recommended it.

## The arguments against, or for a slower approach

- The sudden price rise hit poor households hardest.
- Inflation surged, squeezing incomes.
- Support measures for vulnerable people took time to reach them.

## What it means for the diaspora

Many Nigerians abroad noticed the impact immediately: more requests for help with transport, food and school fees. Budgeting for family support and being clear about what you can afford became even more important.

## Local refining

The opening of large new refining capacity in Nigeria has been widely discussed as a way to reduce dependence on imported fuel. How much it lowers prices over time remains to be seen.

## The bottom line

Ending the fuel subsidy was one of the biggest economic changes in Nigeria for decades. Its effects are still being felt, at home and in the wallets of Nigerians abroad.

**Quick gist:** To work as a doctor in the UK, you need registration with a licence to practise from the General Medical Council (GMC). Many Nigerian-trained doctors take the Professional and Linguistic Assessments Board (PLAB) test, which has two parts. Other routes include postgraduate qualifications and sponsorship schemes. Requirements change, so always check the GMC website.

## Step 1: check eligibility

Your primary medical qualification must be accepted by the GMC, and you'll need to show English language skills, usually through IELTS Academic or the Occupational English Test (OET) at required scores.

## Step 2: PLAB 1

PLAB 1 is a written exam of multiple-choice questions testing clinical knowledge. It can be taken in several countries, including Nigeria in some periods. Check the GMC for current test locations and dates.

## Step 3: PLAB 2

PLAB 2 is a practical exam (OSCE) taken in the UK. It tests clinical and communication skills in realistic scenarios. You'll need a visa to travel for it, usually a visitor visa.

## Step 4: GMC registration

After passing both parts and meeting other requirements, you can apply for registration with a licence to practise.

## Other routes

- **Recognised postgraduate qualifications** such as some UK royal college memberships
- **Sponsorship schemes** through approved organisations
- **Specialist or GP registration** for experienced doctors with eligible qualifications

## Finding a job

Many international doctors start in non-training posts (sometimes called trust grade or locally employed doctor roles) before applying for training programmes. The NHS Jobs website lists posts. Competition for training places has increased, so research carefully.

## The visa

Most doctors come on the Health and Care Worker visa, which has lower fees and an exemption from the Immigration Health Surcharge.

## Tips

- Join study groups and forums for PLAB preparation.
- Budget for exam fees, travel and living costs during PLAB 2.
- Read job descriptions carefully and tailor applications.
- Connect with Nigerian doctors' associations in the UK for mentoring.

## The bottom line

The route to the NHS is well-travelled but demanding. Plan your exams, finances and job search carefully, and use official GMC guidance.

## Research the market

- Look at similar job adverts and salary ranges.
- Use salary guides from recruitment agencies.
- Check official earnings data for your occupation.

## Know your number

Decide three figures:

- **Ideal:** what you'd love
- **Target:** what's fair for your skills
- **Walk-away:** the minimum you'd accept

- Start date

- Training budget
- Extra holiday
- Relocation support

If you need sponsorship, the salary must meet the visa threshold for your role. Make sure the final offer does.

Experience abroad counts. Explain the scale and responsibilities of your previous roles.

## The bottom line

Negotiating is normal and expected. Research, be polite, use evidence and consider the whole package.

**Quick gist:** Scammers often target people in diaspora communities, using trust, urgency and promises of easy money. The most common types are fake investments, romance scams, impersonation of relatives or officials, and fake job offers. Pause, verify and never send money under pressure.

## Common scams

- **Investment scams:** crypto, forex or "high-yield" schemes promoted by friends or influencers, promising guaranteed returns.
- **Romance scams:** someone you meet online builds a relationship, then asks for money for emergencies, travel or investments.
- **"Family emergency" scams:** a message from a new number claiming to be a relative who needs money urgently.
- **Impersonation:** calls pretending to be HMRC, the Home Office, the police or your bank, threatening arrest or deportation.
- **Fake job and visa offers:** demands for fees for sponsorship or visas.

## Warning signs

- Pressure to act quickly
- Requests for payment by crypto, gift cards or unusual transfers
- Promises of guaranteed or very high returns
- Requests to keep it secret
- Stories that change or don't add up

## How to protect yourself

- **Verify:** call the person or organisation using a number you know is real.
- **Check firms:** use the FCA register for UK financial firms.
- **Slow down:** genuine organisations won't force instant payment.
- **Protect your details:** never share PINs, passwords or one-time codes.
- **Talk to someone:** a trusted friend can spot red flags you miss.

## Money mules

Be very careful if someone asks to use your bank account to receive money. It may be money laundering, and you could face criminal charges and lose your bank account.

## If you've been scammed

- Contact your bank immediately.
- Report it to Action Fraud.
- Keep all evidence.
- Don't pay "recovery" companies that promise to get your money back for a fee; many are scams too.

## The bottom line

If it's urgent, secret or too good to be true, stop and check. A five-minute call can save you thousands.

**Quick gist:** Nigerian traditional dress varies by region and ethnic group, and it's a big part of celebrations at home and abroad. Yoruba outfits often feature aso oke and agbada, Igbo ceremonies feature George wrappers and isiagu tops, and Hausa-Fulani styles include flowing babbanriga robes. At diaspora events, coordinated aso ebi fabrics bring families together.

## Yoruba styles

- **Aso oke:** a hand-woven fabric used for gele (head ties), iro and buba (wrapper and blouse), and men's outfits. Often worn at weddings.
- **Agbada:** a wide, flowing robe for men, worn over a buba and sokoto (trousers), usually with a cap (fila).

## Igbo styles

- **Isiagu:** a patterned top, often with lion head designs, worn by men with a red cap for traditional titles.
- **George wrapper:** a rich, often embroidered fabric worn by women, paired with a blouse and head tie.

## Hausa-Fulani styles

- **Babbanriga:** a large, flowing robe often heavily embroidered.
- **Kaftans and caps:** everyday and formal wear.

## Other regions

Nigeria has hundreds of ethnic groups with their own styles, from Edo coral beads and wrappers to the Efik and Ibibio's distinctive outfits.

## Aso ebi abroad

At weddings and parties, families and friends wear matching fabric (aso ebi). It shows support for the celebrants and creates beautiful photos. Order early, as tailors get busy before big events.

## Where to buy abroad

African fabric shops, online stores and tailors in diaspora communities. Many people order from Nigeria and have outfits sewn abroad.

## Teaching children

Dressing children in traditional outfits for cultural days and celebrations helps them feel proud of their heritage.

## The bottom line

Nigerian traditional dress is colourful, diverse and full of meaning. At your next owambe, wear it with pride.

The dish is thought to originate from the Wolof people of the Senegambia region. Nigerian jollof is often made with long-grain parboiled rice and a smoky "party" flavour, while Ghanaian jollof often uses fragrant basmati or jasmine rice.

## Where jollof comes from

Many historians link jollof to thieboudienne, a rice dish from the Wolof people of Senegal. The name "jollof" is thought to come from the Wolof Empire. Over time, the dish spread across West Africa and each country made it its own.

## The Nigerian style

- Long-grain parboiled rice
- A blended base of tomatoes, peppers and onions
- Thyme, curry powder and bay leaves
- The famous smoky "party jollof" flavour from cooking over firewood or letting the bottom slightly burn

## The Ghanaian style

- Often uses basmati or jasmine rice
- Rich spices, sometimes including shito or other flavours
- Often served with shito, salad and grilled meat

## The rivalry

The debate plays out on social media, at parties and in cook-offs. Celebrities, chefs and comedians join in. It's mostly good-natured and has become part of West African pop culture.

## Jollof abroad

In London, Houston and Toronto, jollof is at every African party, and restaurants compete to make the best version. World Jollof Day celebrations have been held in some cities.

## Who wins?

Every Nigerian knows the answer. Every Ghanaian knows a different one. The real winner is everyone who gets a plate.

## The bottom line

The jollof wars are one of West Africa's most delicious rivalries. Make a pot, invite a Ghanaian friend and debate it over dinner.

**Quick gist:** Canadian citizens and permanent residents can sponsor their spouse, common-law partner or conjugal partner for permanent residence. The sponsor must meet eligibility rules and commit to supporting the partner financially. The couple must prove a genuine relationship.

## Who can sponsor

Generally, the sponsor must:

- Be 18 or older
- Be a Canadian citizen, permanent resident or registered Indian under the Indian Act
- Not be receiving social assistance (except for disability)
- Meet other eligibility rules, including no recent sponsorship defaults

## Types of relationship

- **Spouse:** legally married
- **Common-law partner:** living together for at least one year
- **Conjugal partner:** in a committed relationship for at least one year but unable to live together due to circumstances beyond your control

## Inland vs outland

- **Outland:** the partner applies from outside Canada.
- **Inland:** the partner already lives in Canada with the sponsor. In some cases, they may get an open work permit while the application is processed.

## Proving the relationship

Evidence can include:

- Marriage certificate
- Photos over time
- Communication records
- Joint finances or property
- Evidence of visits and shared life

## The sponsorship undertaking

The sponsor signs an undertaking to financially support the partner for a set period. This applies even if the relationship ends.

## Processing

Processing times vary. Check IRCC's website for current estimates. Biometrics, medicals and police certificates are usually required.

## Common pitfalls

- Inconsistent information
- Weak relationship evidence
- Missing documents
- Using unlicensed consultants

## The bottom line

Spousal sponsorship is a well-established route. Gather strong evidence, be consistent and use only licensed consultants or lawyers if you need help.

## Winter (December to February)

Cold, wet and dark, with occasional snow and frost.

- Warm, waterproof coat with a hood
- Thermal base layers
- Jumpers and fleeces
- Gloves, scarf and hat
- Waterproof boots with good grip
- Warm socks

## Spring (March to May)

Mild but unpredictable. Sunshine and showers can change quickly.

- Light jacket or raincoat
- Layers you can remove
- An umbrella in your bag

## Summer (June to August)

Usually mild to warm, with occasional heatwaves. Evenings can be cool.

- Light clothes
- A light jacket for evenings
- Sunscreen; the UK sun can still burn

## Autumn (September to November)

Cooler, windy and wet as the days get shorter.

- Waterproof jacket
- Jumpers
- Boots

## Save money on clothes

- Buy winter coats in end-of-season sales.

- Ask friends and church members; many people pass on clothes.

- Vitamin D: the NHS advises many people to consider vitamin D supplements in autumn and winter, and people with darker skin may need to pay particular attention.

- Stay active indoors when it's dark and wet.

## The bottom line

Layers, waterproofs and good shoes will get you through any UK season. Check the forecast and never leave home without an umbrella.

**Quick gist:** Nigeria is one of the biggest recipients of remittances in Africa, with Nigerians abroad sending close to $20 billion a year home in recent years, according to World Bank estimates. That money supports families, small businesses and the wider economy, and policymakers have been trying to channel more of it through official systems.

## Why remittances matter

- **Family support:** school fees, rent, food, healthcare and weddings.
- **Investment:** property, small businesses and farms.
- **Foreign currency:** remittances bring dollars, pounds and euros into the economy, supporting the naira.
- **Stability:** remittances tend to be steadier than foreign investment, which can leave quickly.

## Official vs informal channels

A lot of money has historically moved through informal channels, partly because of exchange rate differences. Recent reforms, including changes to how international money transfer operators work and how recipients are paid, aim to make official channels more attractive.

## What changed for senders

Under current rules, recipients generally get remittances in naira, and international money transfer operators must be licensed. Exchange rates have become more market-based, narrowing the gap with informal rates at times.

## Diaspora investment

The government and private sector have tried to attract diaspora investment through products like diaspora bonds, mortgage schemes and investment platforms. Results have been mixed, often because of trust and currency concerns.

## The bigger picture

Remittances help families survive tough economic times, but they can't replace jobs, infrastructure and good governance. Many diaspora Nigerians want policies that make it easier and safer to invest at home.

## The bottom line

Nigerians abroad are a major economic force for Nigeria. Using regulated channels protects your money and helps strengthen the official system.
