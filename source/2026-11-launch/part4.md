---
**Quick gist:** Dress in layers, keep at least one room properly warm, take vitamin D from October to March as the NHS suggests, deal with damp early, get a proper coat and boots, and plan for the darkness. Your first winter is the hardest. After that, you'll know the drill.

You've seen the memes. Then the clocks go back, it's pitch dark at 4:30pm, the wind is coming at you sideways and Lagos traffic suddenly seems romantic. Welcome to your first British winter. Here's how to handle it.

## 1. Layers beat one big jumper

The trick is **layers**: a thin thermal base layer, a regular top, a jumper or fleece and a proper **waterproof coat**. Then add gloves, a scarf and a hat. You lose a lot of heat through your head and hands, and no one will laugh at your beanie.

**Shoes matter.** Canvas sneakers and wet pavements don't mix. Get waterproof boots with a decent grip, because ice is no joke.

## 2. Heating: warm one room well

Energy bills are a real worry. A few tips:

- **Heat the rooms you use,** and close doors to keep the heat in.
- The NHS suggests keeping the rooms you use regularly at **18°C or higher**, especially if you have health conditions or young children.
- **Draught-proof** windows and doors with cheap stick-on strips.
- **Thick curtains** closed at dusk make a big difference.
- **Hot water bottles and electric blankets** are cheap to run compared with heating the whole house.

If you're struggling to pay, contact your energy supplier early. Ofgem has guidance on help schemes, and suppliers have to work with you.

## 3. Damp and mould: deal with it early

Cold weather plus drying clothes indoors often means **condensation**, which leads to black mould. To prevent it:

- Open windows briefly each day, even when it's cold.
- Use extractor fans when cooking and showering.
- Wipe condensation off windows in the morning.
- If you're renting and mould keeps coming back, **report it to your landlord in writing**. Landlords have legal duties on damp and mould.

## 4. Vitamin D: the one nobody tells you about

With less sunlight, the NHS advises everyone to consider a daily **10 microgram vitamin D supplement from October to March**. People with darker skin may not make enough vitamin D from sunlight even in summer, so the NHS suggests some may need it all year. Supermarket brands are cheap.

## 5. The darkness is real

Short days hit mood hard, especially when you're far from family. What helps:

- **Get outside in daylight,** even for a 20-minute lunchtime walk.
- **Plan things to look forward to:** church, community events, Naija parties, Sunday rice. 😄
- **Call home on video,** not just voice notes.
- If you feel persistently low, talk to your **GP**. Low mood in winter is common and treatable, and it's nothing to be ashamed of.

## 6. Get registered with a GP now

Flu season peaks in winter. Register with a local GP surgery as soon as you can. Ask whether you're eligible for a **free flu jab**, as some groups are.

## 7. Plan your travel around the weather

Trains get delayed, buses get cancelled and snow can shut things down. Check travel apps before you leave, and give yourself extra time for work or school.

## You'll be fine

By your second winter, you'll be the one sending this to the new arrivals. Stay warm. 🧣

*General information only, not medical advice. Speak to your GP or pharmacist about your own health.*

title: Where to find Nigerian food in the UK: a beginner's guide for new japa people
slug: where-to-find-nigerian-food-in-the-uk
category: Diaspora Life
tags: food, UK life, African shops, cooking
excerpt: Craving egusi, ponmo or proper Nigerian rice? Here's where to find Naija ingredients in the UK, what to swap when you can't, and how to save money doing it.
sources: GOV.UK — Bringing food into Great Britain|https://www.gov.uk/bringing-food-into-great-britain
---
**Quick gist:** Most UK towns with a sizeable African community have at least one African or "world foods" shop. Big supermarkets now stock plantain, yam, scotch bonnets and some African brands. Online African grocers deliver nationwide. And be careful what you pack from home: the UK has strict rules on bringing in meat and dairy.

The first few weeks abroad, you'll eat a lot of sandwiches and pretend you're fine. Then one day you'll be craving pepper soup so much it hurts. Here's how to find home on a plate.

## 1. African and Caribbean shops

Search "African shop near me" or "African food store" on Google Maps. Most cities, and many big towns, have at least one. They usually stock:

- **Grains and flours:** garri, semovita, poundo, amala flour, beans (oloyin, brown, black-eyed)
- **Soup ingredients:** egusi, ogbono, crayfish, dried fish, stockfish, uziza, ugu (sometimes frozen), bitter leaf
- **Proteins:** ponmo, goat meat, snails (seasonal), frozen fish
- **Snacks and drinks:** chin chin, plantain chips, Malt and Nigerian biscuits

**Tip:** prices vary a lot between shops, so compare. And ask the shop owner; they often know the community WhatsApp groups and events.

## 2. Supermarkets are catching up

Big chains in diverse areas now carry:

- **Plantain** (ripe and unripe), **yam** and **sweet potatoes**
- **Scotch bonnet peppers**, which are perfect for your pepper mix
- **Long grain parboiled rice** for jollof
- A "world foods" aisle with some West African brands, seasonings and palm oil

Discount supermarkets can be great for onions, tomatoes and chicken, the backbone of most Naija stews.

## 3. Online African grocers

If there's no shop nearby, there are many UK-based online African grocers that deliver nationwide. Order in bulk with friends or church members to split delivery costs.

## 4. Smart swaps when you can't find something

- **Ugu (pumpkin leaf):** frozen ugu, or spinach as a backup
- **Bitter leaf:** dried or frozen bitter leaf travels well
- **Fresh pepper mix:** blend scotch bonnets, tatashe (red bell peppers), tomatoes and onions, then freeze in portions
- **Locust beans (iru):** dried or frozen iru is usually available in African shops

## 5. Be careful what you bring from Nigeria

It's tempting to fill your suitcase with dried fish and meat. But the UK has **strict rules** on bringing in meat, dairy and some other animal products from outside the EU. Items can be seized at the border, and you can be fined. Many dried plant items such as spices, grains and dried leaves are generally fine. Check the GOV.UK page on bringing food into Great Britain before you travel.

## 6. Nigerian restaurants and buka spots

London, Manchester, Birmingham and other big cities have Nigerian restaurants, from casual buka-style spots to fancier places. They're great for a taste of home and for meeting other Nigerians. Check Google reviews and community recommendations.

## Save money: cook in batches

Cook a big pot of stew on Sunday, portion it out and freeze it. It's cheaper, healthier and it beats queuing for overpriced takeaway jollof. 😉

title: The japa checklist: 15 things to sort out before you leave Nigeria
slug: japa-checklist-15-things-before-leaving-nigeria
category: Diaspora Life
tags: japa, relocation, checklist, documents
excerpt: Documents, money, phone, health and family. This is the checklist we wish someone had given us before our flight.
sources: Nigeria Immigration Service — Passport services|https://immigration.gov.ng/; NIMC — National Identity Management Commission|https://nimc.gov.ng/
---
**Quick gist:** Before you japa, sort your documents (originals plus certified copies), keep your Nigerian bank accounts, BVN and NIN active, get transcripts and references, arrange how money will move, do a full health and dental check, and leave clear plans for family and property. Things that take five minutes in Lagos can take five months from abroad.

You've got your visa. Congratulations! 🎉 Now comes the part people underestimate: preparing to leave properly. Here's the checklist.

## Documents

1. **Passport with plenty of validity.** Renewing from abroad is possible but slower. If your passport expires within the next couple of years, consider renewing before you go.
2. **Certified copies of everything:** birth certificate, degree certificates, transcripts, marriage certificate, children's birth certificates and any change-of-name documents. Scan them all to secure cloud storage too.
3. **Academic transcripts sent officially.** If you'll need them for jobs or further study abroad, request them now. Universities can be very slow.
4. **Professional registrations and references.** Nurses, doctors, engineers and teachers: get letters of good standing and work references while your old bosses still remember you.
5. **Driving history.** Some countries take your Nigerian driving history into account for insurance. Get a letter from FRSC or keep your licence records.

## Money

6. **Keep your Nigerian accounts active.** Don't close them. Update your phone number and make sure internet banking works from abroad.
7. **Link your BVN and NIN** and make sure the details match your passport exactly. Mismatched names cause endless problems.
8. **Arrange how money will move.** Decide how you'll send money home and who will handle payments at home.
9. **Bring proof of funds and bank statements** for your first weeks. Landlords and banks abroad may ask.

## Phone and digital life

10. **Keep your Nigerian SIM active.** Many banks still send OTPs by SMS. Set up a roaming plan or ask a trusted person to keep the SIM in a phone at home.
11. **Turn on two-factor authentication** on email and social media using an authenticator app, not just SMS.

## Health

12. **Full medical, dental and eye check.** Dental work and glasses can cost much more abroad, and you may wait for appointments.
13. **Bring a record of your vaccinations** and any prescriptions, plus a short supply of regular medication. Check the destination's rules for bringing medicines.

## Family and property

14. **Leave clear instructions.** If you own land or property, appoint someone you trust, ideally with a proper legal power of attorney. Many diaspora Nigerians have lost land to "family arrangements".
15. **Have the honest money conversation.** Agree with family what support you can realistically send, especially in your first six months when costs are highest and income may not have started.

## Bonus: the first-week bag

Pack your first week as if your suitcase gets lost: documents, chargers, a change of clothes, medication and a warm layer in your hand luggage.

title: Building a UK credit score from zero: a guide for new arrivals
slug: build-uk-credit-score-from-zero-new-arrivals
category: Money
tags: credit score, UK life, banking, renting
excerpt: Your Nigerian credit history doesn't follow you to the UK. Here's how to build a UK credit file from scratch so you can rent, get a phone contract and eventually a mortgage.
sources: MoneyHelper — How to improve your credit score|https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/everyday-money/credit-and-purchases/how-to-improve-your-credit-score; GOV.UK — Register to vote|https://www.gov.uk/register-to-vote
---
**Quick gist:** You arrive in the UK with no credit file. Build one by opening a UK bank account, getting on the electoral register if you're eligible, using a credit-builder card for small purchases and **paying it off in full every month**, putting bills in your name and avoiding lots of applications in a short time. It takes about 6–12 months to see real progress.

In Nigeria, you might have been doing well, with a car loan paid off and a good relationship with your bank. In the UK, none of that counts. To the UK credit agencies, you're a blank page. Here's how to fill it.

## Why it matters

Your credit file affects whether you can get:

- a **phone contract** rather than pay-as-you-go
- a **rental flat** without a huge deposit or guarantor
- a **car on finance**
- eventually, a **mortgage**

The UK has three main credit reference agencies: **Experian**, **Equifax** and **TransUnion**. Each keeps its own file on you, and lenders may check any of them.

## Step 1: Open a UK bank account

A current account is the foundation. Many digital banks let you open one with just your passport and visa, sometimes before you have proof of address. Use it for your salary and regular spending.

## Step 2: Get on the electoral register (if you can)

Being on the electoral roll at your address is one of the easiest ways to show lenders who you are and where you live. **Commonwealth citizens**, which includes Nigerians, with permission to be in the UK can usually register to vote. Check your eligibility on GOV.UK. It's free and takes minutes.

## Step 3: Use a credit-builder card the right way

Credit-builder cards have low limits and higher interest. That's fine, because you're **never going to pay interest**:

1. Use it for **one small regular purchase**, such as a phone top-up or a weekly shop.
2. Set up a **direct debit to pay the full balance every month**.
3. Keep your balance **well below the limit**. Many lenders like to see usage under about 30%.

Do this for six months and your file starts to look healthy.

## Step 4: Put bills in your name

Council tax, energy, broadband and your phone contract all help show you're a reliable payer. Some services also let you add rent payments to your credit file.

## Step 5: Avoid these mistakes

- **Don't apply for lots of credit at once.** Each full application leaves a "hard search" on your file. Use **eligibility checkers**, which do soft searches, first.
- **Never miss a payment.** One missed payment can stay on your file for six years.
- **Don't max out your card,** even if you pay it off later.
- **Be careful with "buy now, pay later".** It's now increasingly reported to credit agencies.

## Check your file for free

You can check your credit file free through services linked to each agency. Look out for errors, like wrong addresses or accounts that aren't yours, and get them corrected.

## Be patient

Credit is built over time. Do the basics consistently, and within a year you'll be in a much stronger position.

*General information, not financial advice. MoneyHelper, backed by the UK government, offers free guidance.*

title: Raising Naija kids abroad: how to keep the culture (and the language) alive
slug: raising-nigerian-kids-abroad-culture-language
category: Diaspora Life
tags: parenting, culture, language, family
excerpt: Your children may grow up with British, Canadian or American accents. Here's how to make sure they still know where they come from.
sources: UNESCO — Why mother language-based education is essential|https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/why-mother-language-based-education-essential
---
**Quick gist:** Speak your language at home every day, even if they reply in English. Make Nigerian food, music and stories normal rather than "special occasion". Connect them with grandparents by video regularly. Find community: church, cultural associations and Saturday schools. And when you can afford it, take them home.

One day your child will say "Mum, can we have *pasta* instead?" in a perfect British accent, and you'll realise the culture won't pass on by itself. Here's how many diaspora parents keep it alive.

## 1. Speak the language at home, consistently

This is the one most of us struggle with. It feels easier to switch to English, especially when they answer in English. But children pick up languages they **hear daily**, even if they don't speak them back at first.

- Pick **one language at home** (Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Pidgin or another) and stick with it, at least at meals and bedtime.
- Don't panic if they reply in English. **Understanding comes first**, and speaking follows.
- Use **songs, cartoons and YouTube channels** in your language. There are more than ever.

UNESCO and many researchers note that children benefit from keeping their home language. It doesn't hold back their English.

## 2. Make Naija food everyday food

If jollof only appears at parties, it becomes "party food" rather than "our food". Cook Nigerian meals during the normal week. Let them help: washing beans, frying plantain, stirring the pot. Food is memory.

## 3. Grandparents on video, regularly

Weekly video calls with grandma and grandpa build a bond that can last a lifetime. Let the grandparents tell stories, sing the old songs and ask about school. Even if it's chaotic, it matters.

## 4. Find your community

- **Churches and mosques** with large Nigerian congregations
- **Cultural associations**, such as state or ethnic unions, often organised by town
- **Saturday schools** that teach language, history and culture
- **Naija playdates** with other diaspora families, so it's not just *your* weird mum doing it 😄

## 5. Teach them the stories

Tell them about your village, your school and what Christmas was like at home. Teach them proverbs and folk tales. Show them old family photos. Children are proud of what they understand.

## 6. Celebrate both identities

Your child can be fully British, Canadian or American **and** fully Nigerian. Avoid making them feel they have to choose. Celebrate Independence Day on 1 October and Bonfire Night, Thanksgiving or Canada Day too.

## 7. Take them home

Nothing beats the real thing. Even one trip every few years, to see the markets, hear the noise and eat agege bread from the roadside, can anchor their identity for life. Start saving early; it's worth it.

title: How to land your first UK job: CV tips every Nigerian professional should know
slug: first-uk-job-cv-tips-nigerian-professionals
category: Jobs & Careers
tags: jobs, CV, UK, career, Skilled Worker
excerpt: A CV that works in Lagos can get ignored in London. Here's how to rewrite yours for UK employers and get past the first screening.
sources: National Careers Service — CV sections|https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections; GOV.UK — Register of licensed sponsors: workers|https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/register-of-licensed-sponsors-workers
---
**Quick gist:** UK CVs are **two pages**, with **no photo, no date of birth, no marital status and no religion**. Lead with a short profile, then show achievements with numbers. Tailor your CV for every job. If you need sponsorship, target employers on the government's sponsor register and say clearly what your right to work is.

Many skilled Nigerians arrive in the UK with great experience and still get silence from employers. Often the problem isn't you, it's your CV format. Here's how to fix it.

## 1. Remove what UK employers don't want

A typical Nigerian CV often includes:

- ❌ Passport photo
- ❌ Date of birth or age
- ❌ Marital status, number of children
- ❌ State of origin, religion
- ❌ "Referees available" with full contact details on page one

In the UK, employers **don't ask for these**, partly because of equality and anti-discrimination rules. Including them can make your CV look out of place. Remove them.

## 2. Keep it to two pages

UK recruiters skim. **Two pages** is standard for most roles. Put the most recent and relevant experience first.

## 3. Start with a sharp profile

Three or four lines at the top that say **who you are, what you do and what you're looking for**:

> *Chartered accountant with 7 years' experience in audit and financial reporting across banking and telecoms. ACCA-qualified, skilled in IFRS and SAP. Seeking a financial reporting role in the NHS or public sector.*

## 4. Achievements, not duties

Compare these:

- ❌ "Responsible for managing the sales team."
- ✅ "Led a team of 8 to grow regional sales by 32% in 12 months."

Use **numbers** wherever possible: money saved, targets hit, people managed, processes improved.

## 5. Translate your experience

UK employers may not know Nigerian companies or qualifications. Add context:

- "*Dangote Group (one of Africa's largest industrial conglomerates)*"
- If useful, get your qualifications compared through the UK's official recognition service (**UK ENIC**) and mention it.

## 6. Tailor it for every application

Read the job advert and **use its key words** in your CV, especially the skills listed under "essential criteria". Many employers use software to screen CVs before a human sees them.

## 7. Be clear about your right to work

- If you have the right to work (for example, a Graduate visa, dependant visa or ILR), say so briefly: *"Full right to work in the UK (Graduate visa, valid to 2028)."*
- If you **need sponsorship**, target employers on the **GOV.UK register of licensed sponsors**. It lists every organisation that can sponsor Skilled Worker visas.

## 8. Write a short cover letter

For many jobs, especially in the public sector and NHS, the **supporting statement** matters as much as the CV. Address each essential criterion with a real example.

## 9. Use LinkedIn properly

Recruiters search LinkedIn constantly. Use a professional photo (yes, the photo goes on LinkedIn, not your CV), a clear headline and the same achievements as your CV.

## Don't give up

Rejections are part of the process, even for people who were senior at home. Many Nigerians start a step lower and climb quickly once they have UK experience. Keep applying, keep improving and lean on your network.
