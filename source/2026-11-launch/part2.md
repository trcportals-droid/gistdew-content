**Quick gist:** Your UK immigration status now lives online as an **eVisa**. To see it and share it with employers, landlords or airlines, you need a **UKVI account** linked to your eVisa. It takes three steps: create the account, confirm your identity with the "UK Immigration: ID Check" app, and link your eVisa. UKVI has been reminding people of the process again this month.

If you've been putting this off, today's the day. Without access to your eVisa you can have trouble at the airport, with a new job or with a new tenancy.

## Do you already have a UKVI account?

Check before you create a new one. You **already have one** if:

- you used the UK Immigration: ID Check app when you applied for your visa, or
- you got a "UKVI account confirmation" email when you applied, or
- you applied to the EU Settlement Scheme.

If any of those apply, sign in instead. **Never create a second account.** It causes problems that take weeks to untangle.

## What you'll need

- Your **passport** or your **BRP** (an expired BRP can be used for up to 24 months after its expiry date, or until 31 December 2026, whichever comes first)
- An **email address** and **phone number** you'll keep long term
- A **smartphone** for the ID Check app. You can borrow a friend's phone for this step, and the app can be deleted afterwards.
- Ideally a **laptop or tablet** as well, because you'll switch between the website and the app

## Step 1: Create your UKVI account

Go to **gov.uk/get-access-evisa** and choose to create an account. Enter your details **exactly** as they appear on your identity document, and check the date format carefully before you submit. You'll get a confirmation email.

## Step 2: Confirm your identity

You'll be asked to download the **UK Immigration: ID Check** app. Use it to:

1. Scan your passport or BRP. **Use the same document** you chose when creating the account.
2. Scan the chip in your document by holding your phone against it.
3. Take a selfie. Treat it like a passport photo: plain background, no smile, no glasses.

If the app won't read your document, try another phone or take the case off. If you created the account with an old passport, you may need to add your new passport first.

## Step 3: Link your eVisa

Follow the on-screen steps to link your account to your eVisa. You'll be asked a few security questions. You'll get an email once it's linked. Sign in and **check every detail**: your name, date of birth, visa type and expiry date.

## Families: everyone needs their own account

Each dependant, including your spouse and every child, needs their **own** UKVI account and eVisa link. Parents can set these up for children.

## Before you travel

- Make sure your **current passport** is linked to your UKVI account. If you renewed your Nigerian passport, add the new one **before** you fly.
- Generate a **share code** if an employer or landlord asks for proof of status. Share codes expire, so create a fresh one each time.

## Stuck?

GOV.UK has short help videos for every scenario, including what to do if you have no identity document at all. There's also a UKVI Resolution Centre phone line if you can't complete the steps online.

*This guide is general information. Always follow the latest instructions on GOV.UK.*

## Official vs parallel: does it still matter?

A few years ago the gap was huge and many people went through informal channels. Today the difference is only a few percent. And since **May 2026**, CBN rules require licensed money transfer companies to pay out in **naira**, priced at market rates.

That means a good transfer app usually gets you close to the street rate, with:

- **no cash-handling risk**
- **a proper record** of the transfer, which matters if you ever need to show source of funds
- **no risk of being caught up in a fraud investigation** with an unknown dealer

## How to get the most naira for your pounds

1. **Compare the final amount received, not just the rate.** Some apps advertise a great rate and hide a fee. Others charge no fee but use a weaker rate.
2. **Check two or three apps before every big transfer.** Rates change daily and the best app this week might not be the best next week.
3. **Send larger amounts less often.** Fixed fees hurt small transfers most.
4. **Avoid sending on weekends** if you can. Some providers widen their margins when markets are closed.
5. **Watch for promotions.** Many apps give better rates on your first transfer, so new users can save a little.

## Sending for December?

December demand usually pushes rates around and fills family WhatsApp groups with requests. If you know what you'll send for the festive season, consider sending in early December rather than the week before Christmas.

**Quick gist:** Since **1 May 2026**, licensed money transfer operators (IMTOs) such as the big transfer apps must route remittances through naira settlement accounts at Nigerian banks and price them at live market rates. Your family receives **naira**, not dollars. In **November 2026**, the CBN said crypto sent home as a personal remittance must also go through a licensed operator, with the receiver paid in naira.

If you've noticed that your mum can no longer receive dollars through your usual app, this is why.

## The May 2026 rules in plain English

The CBN issued a circular on 24 March 2026, which took effect on 1 May 2026. The key points:

- **Naira settlement accounts:** every licensed transfer company must process payouts through designated naira accounts at authorised Nigerian banks.
- **Market pricing:** transfer companies must set their rates using live market prices rather than rates they pick themselves.
- **Stricter monitoring:** every transaction is more traceable, as part of the CBN's push to bring diaspora money into the formal system.

## What it means for you as a sender

- **Your family gets naira.** If they relied on receiving dollars to save in dollars, that route through transfer apps has largely closed.
- **Rates should be fairer and more transparent.** Because transfer companies have to price close to the market, the gap between app rates and street rates has narrowed.
- **More checks.** Expect identity checks and occasional questions about large or frequent transfers. That's normal, not a sign you've done anything wrong.

## What changed for crypto in November

Many Nigerians abroad started sending USDT or USDC home to get around naira payouts. On 5 November 2026 the CBN issued new regulations covering virtual assets. A payout guide published on 13 November says an inbound personal remittance using crypto may now only be handled by a **licensed IMTO**, or a licensee working with one, and the person receiving it is **paid in naira**.

In short, sending crypto peer-to-peer as a "remittance" now sits in a grey area. If you send crypto home, understand that the rules have tightened and your recipient could face questions from their bank.

## Practical tips

1. **Use licensed providers.** It protects you and your family.
2. **Keep records** of what you send and why: school fees, upkeep, building. It helps if a bank ever asks.
3. **Paying school fees abroad for a sibling?** The CBN raised the tuition remittance cap to $25,000 per semester, but that is for payments *out* of Nigeria through banks, a different channel.
4. **Want to save in dollars for family?** Talk to a Nigerian bank about domiciliary account options, which are separate from transfer-app payouts.

title: Sending money home: 7 mistakes that cost Nigerians abroad thousands every year

**Quick gist:** Most people lose money on transfers through hidden exchange-rate margins, small frequent sends and no budget for family support. Compare the amount received, batch your transfers, agree a monthly figure with family, and keep records.

Supporting family back home is part of life for most of us abroad. But between fees, poor rates and endless "urgent" requests, a lot of hard-earned money leaks away. Here are the seven mistakes we see most, and how to fix them.

## 1. Comparing the rate, not the amount received

An app might show a shiny rate but charge a £3.99 fee, while another has no fee and a slightly weaker rate. The only number that matters is **how much naira lands in the account** for the pounds you put in. Always compare that.

## 2. Sending small amounts many times

Fixed fees eat small transfers alive. Sending £30 four times a month can cost several times more in fees than sending £120 once. Where you can, **batch your sends**.

## 3. Using your bank's international transfer

High-street banks often charge a flat fee plus a poor exchange rate. For most people, a specialist transfer service is much cheaper. The World Bank tracks remittance costs worldwide, and banks are consistently among the most expensive channels.

## 4. No monthly "family support" budget

If every request is treated as a surprise, you'll never know how much you actually send. Sit down once and set a **fixed monthly figure** for family support. Share it with whoever coordinates at home. It's kinder for everyone than yes-no-yes-no every week.

## 5. Sending to the wrong account in a rush

Mistyped account numbers, especially with similar bank names, are common and painful to reverse. **Save trusted recipients** in your app and double-check new ones. Never send to a new account just because a WhatsApp message asked you to. Call the person first, because family accounts get hacked too.

## 6. Ignoring the rules

Since May 2026, licensed transfer companies pay out in naira at market rates. Using unlicensed dealers or random crypto traders to "beat the rate" can leave your family's account frozen. The few naira you save aren't worth it.

## 7. Paying "black tax" with no plan for yourself

Supporting family is honourable, but not at the expense of your rent, emergency fund and pension. Financial advisers often suggest building **three months of expenses** as an emergency fund first. A broke you can't help anyone next year.

## A simple system that works

1. Pick **one main transfer app** and one backup.
2. Set a **monthly family amount** and send it on the same day each month.
3. Keep a small **"emergency home fund"** in a separate pot for genuine emergencies.
4. Keep **records** of what you sent and why.

You'll send the same love with a lot less stress.

*General information, not financial advice.*
